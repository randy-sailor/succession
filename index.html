01The case for planning early

The most predictable event in any company is the one least planned for.

Leadership and ownership both turn over. The data below is consistent across decades of research: intention is high, preparation is low, and the gap is expensive. Every figure links to its source.

~$1T

Market value erased each year by poorly managed CEO and C-suite transitions in the S&P 1500 alone. Better planning is linked to valuations roughly 20–25% higher.

Fernández-Aráoz, Green & Nagel · HBR, 2021

20–30%

Share of businesses that go to market and actually sell, leaving up to 80% of owners unable to harvest their wealth on the way out.

Exit Planning Institute · State of Owner Readiness

~51%

Of the U.S. business market is owned by Baby Boomers set to transition within ten years: the "Silver Tsunami." Estimates of value changing hands run into the trillions.

EPI · Harvard BiGS, 2025

The 5 D's

Roughly half of all transitions are forced, not chosen, triggered by one of five events: death, disability, divorce, disagreement, or distress. The plan you build calmly is the plan that survives them.

EPI, via Rehmann & Focus Partners

The paradox

78% of family business leaders expect a CEO transition within a decade and 85% call planning critical, yet only 57% have a plan and just 23% are actively implementing one. 30% say they are already behind schedule.

Deloitte Private survey of 300 executives · Feb 2026

30 / 12 / 3%

The often-cited share of family firms surviving to the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th generation. Useful as a warning, but treat with care: the original research measured "through" not "to" a generation and is frequently misquoted.

Family Business Institute / Ward · see the critical read

5.7pts

Average drop in shareholder returns at family-owned businesses in the five years after a CEO transition versus the five years before. The usual culprit is not the heir: it is an outgoing CEO who does not plan the exit.

McKinsey, 200+ firms · via Fortune, 2026

~75%

Of owners who do sell report profound regret within a year, most often because value, identity, and life-after were never prepared. The deal closes; the readiness was missing.

EPI, via Rehmann

02The two domains of succession

One word. Two disciplines. They must be planned together.

Most plans fail because they solve only half the problem. Internal succession keeps the business running; external succession transfers what it is worth. Neglect either and the other collapses, an owner-dependent company is both leaderless and unsellable.

Internal · Leadership & continuity

Who runs it next

"If the leader is gone Monday, who decides on Tuesday?"

  • Build a bench: identify and develop high-potential successors before you need them
  • Map roles to a leadership pipeline, not a single name on a sticky note
  • Keep an emergency successor named and ready for the unplanned exit
  • Benchmark internal candidates honestly against the outside market
  • Transfer relationships, knowledge, and authority deliberately, not by accident
External · Ownership & liquidity

Who owns it next

"When you stop, how does your wealth get out, and to whom?"

  • Choose a transfer path: family, management, employees, or an outside buyer
  • Maximize transferable value so the company is worth more without you in it
  • Align the personal and financial plan, not just the deal
  • Structure for tax, timing, and the family relationships that outlive the sale
  • Make exit "present tense": review options on a fixed cadence, not at the end

The link between them. Owner dependence is the single most common deal killer. A deep internal bench is what makes the external transfer possible at full value, which is why these two domains are never planned in isolation. Strengthen the leadership pipeline and you raise the sale price at the same time.

The Six Passages

Charan, Drotter & Noel · The Leadership Pipeline

Leadership is not one skill but a series of transitions, from managing self, to others, to managers, to a function, to a business, to a group, to the enterprise. Each passage demands new skills, time horizons, and values. Develop people for the next passage, not just the next title.

Performance × Potential · talent review

04The transfer spectrum

Eight ways out. The right one fits the family, not the headline.

Almost every owner chooses among these paths. Each trades price against control, tax, speed, and legacy differently. The framing question is not "what is it worth" but "what does this business need to do for the family over the next twenty years."

Internal transfer · keeps it in the family / company External transfer · liquidity from outside
PATH 01

Intergenerational transfer Internal

Best when the next generation has both the will and the capability, and legacy outranks price. Highest emotional stakes; needs governance and a real readiness assessment of the heir.

PATH 02

Management buyout Internal

Best when a capable team already runs daily operations. Preserves culture and continuity; the team usually needs seller financing or outside capital to fund it.

PATH 03

Sale to partners / co-owners Internal

Best when shareholders have diverging time horizons. A funded buy-sell agreement is the mechanism, and the one most often missing when a "5 D" hits.

PATH 04

ESOP Internal

Best when you want to reward employees and defer tax. Flexible, partial or full liquidity, preserves legacy, but typically prices below a strategic sale.

PATH 05

Strategic sale External

Best when maximizing price is the goal. Sell to a competitor or larger company that values synergies; usually the highest headline number, least continuity for the team.

PATH 06

Private equity recapitalization External

Best when you want partial liquidity now and a "second bite" later. Take chips off the table, keep equity and a growth partner; record dry powder is chasing these deals.

PATH 07

Initial public offering External

Best when scale, growth, and access to capital markets justify the cost and scrutiny. Rare for mid-market; transformative when it fits.

PATH 08

Orderly liquidation External

Best when the enterprise value lives in the owner and cannot transfer. The fallback, not a plan. The work of value acceleration exists precisely to keep you off this path.

05The protocol

A defensible sequence, drawn from the research.

No single body owns "the" succession process, but the credible methodologies converge on the same spine. This is that consensus, ordered for action and timed against best-practice windows.

1

Start before you think you need to

Begin pipeline development three to ten years out; formalize the transition twelve to eighteen months before the handover. The emergency plan, by contrast, cannot wait, it has to exist before the vacancy.

Window: 3–10 yrs · sources: Egon Zehnder, Harvard Law CG

2

Define the goal across all three legs

Clarify what the owner and family actually want, business, personal, and financial, before choosing a path. Most post-sale regret traces back to skipping the personal and financial legs.

Tool: Three Legs of the Stool · source: EPI Value Acceleration

3

Assess readiness and attractiveness

Run a baseline on two axes: is the business attractive to a buyer or successor, and is the owner personally and financially ready to let go? Calculate the value, profit, and wealth gaps.

Tool: Triggering Event assessment · source: EPI

4

Build the emergency plan now

Name interim leadership, pre-decide authorities and communications, and give emergency candidates board and CEO exposure today. This is the insurance policy against the half of transitions that arrive unplanned.

Owner: board / lead director · source: PwC Governance

5

Identify, develop, and benchmark successors

Map candidates to the leadership pipeline, develop them with real responsibility and board exposure, and benchmark them honestly against the external market. Internal promotion usually creates more value, but only when the bench is genuinely prepared.

Tools: 9-box, pipeline map · source: Russell Reynolds

6

De-risk owner dependence

Document systems, deepen management, and reassign key relationships so the company passes the Two-Week Test. Every point of concentration you remove is value you keep at transfer.

Test: would operations stall without you? · source: Old National

7

Choose the path and structure

Map the eight transfer paths against the family's twenty-year answer, with real after-tax numbers. The wrong path picked for the wrong reason is the most common cause of failed transitions.

Tool: path comparison · source: exit-path mapping

8

Govern, execute, and support the aftermath

Put succession on the board agenda at least annually, keep the final decision with the board rather than the incumbent, and support the successor through the first chapters. A transition is a process with twists, not a one-time event.

Cadence: annual minimum · sources: Deloitte, Fast Company

06Tools, templates & artifacts

The working documents, and where they come from.

A plan is only as good as its artifacts. These are the instruments that turn a methodology into something a board can actually run.

A-01

Readiness & Attractiveness Assessment

The two-axis baseline of owner readiness and business attractiveness, plus the value, profit, and wealth gaps. The "triggering event" that prompts action.

A-02

Emergency Succession Plan

Named interim leaders, pre-cleared authorities, decision rights, and a communications sequence for a sudden vacancy. Board-owned, reviewed annually.

A-03

Success Profile & Competency Model

A clear definition of the role's future requirements and the competencies and values a successor must demonstrate, the spec you hire and develop against.

A-04

9-Box Talent Calibration

The performance-by-potential grid used in a structured talent review to rank readiness, target development, and surface gaps in the bench.

A-05

Leadership Pipeline Map

Each critical role mapped to its pipeline passage, with named candidates and development plans for the next transition, not just the next promotion.

A-06

Two-Week Test Audit

A structured owner-dependence review of quoting, decisions, scheduling, invoicing, and problem-solving, scored to quantify concentration risk.

A-07

Buy-Sell & Governance Docs

Funded buy-sell agreements, operating-agreement transfer provisions, family council and board charters, the legal scaffolding of any transfer.

A-08

Transition Timeline & RACI

A sequenced plan that assigns responsibility for each milestone across owner, board, successor, and advisors, with the handover dates that hold people to it.

A-09

Path Comparison Model

The eight transfer paths run side by side with real after-tax proceeds, control, and continuity outcomes for the specific family situation.

10Questions you are not asking

The questions that separate a plan from a wish.

Most owners ask "what is my business worth." The harder, more useful questions sit underneath that one. Use these to pressure-test where you really are, across every dimension of the handoff.

I.Personal readiness
  • Who are you the day after you no longer run this company?
  • What will you say yes to that the business currently prevents?
  • Have you separated your identity from your title, on paper and in fact?
II.Financial readiness
  • Does the sale actually fund the life you want, after tax?
  • What is your number, and what is the gap between it and today's value?
  • If 80% of your wealth is in the business, what is the diversification plan?
III.Transferable value
  • Would the business pass the Two-Week Test without you tomorrow?
  • Which customer relationships live only in your head or your phone?
  • Is your value concentrated in one client, one vendor, or one person?
IV.The bench
  • Name three people who could run this. Now name their development plans.
  • Have you benchmarked your best internal candidate against the outside market?
  • Are you developing successors, or quietly making yourself indispensable?
V.Contingency
  • If you were gone Monday, who signs, who decides, who tells the team?
  • Is your buy-sell agreement funded, or just signed?
  • Which of the five D's are you least prepared for right now?
VI.Governance & family
  • Does the final decision rest with a board, or with the person leaving?
  • Have you separated ownership, management, and family roles explicitly?
  • What conversation are you avoiding because it will be uncomfortable at Thanksgiving?
VII.Path & structure
  • What does this business need to do for the family over the next twenty years?
  • Have you compared all eight paths with real numbers, or just the obvious one?
  • Are you optimizing for price, for continuity, or for legacy, and does your family agree?
VIII.Timing & cadence
  • If the ideal window is three to ten years out, what have you done this quarter?
  • Is succession a standing item on your board agenda, or an event you are dreading?
  • When did you last "dip your toe" and treat exit as present tense?

The handoff is not the end of the work.
It is the proof of it.

A company that cannot outlive its founder was never finished being built. Plan the transition with the same rigor you brought to building the thing, and the legacy takes care of itself.
